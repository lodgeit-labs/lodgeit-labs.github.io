Mind the Causal Ladder — why financial truth requires a deterministic firewall

What recent peer-reviewed research on Pearl’s Ladder of Causality tells us about latent world models, SBRM, and Logical English.

At LodgeiT Labs, our core architectural premise is simple: probabilistic AI belongs at the perception boundary; statutory and financial truth belongs in deterministic logic.

Across our open-source ecosystem — from ClawDog (our neurosemantic accounting engine pairing LLM flexibility in front with SWI-Prolog rigour behind) to our Logical English statutory rulesets and the Fano Classifier — we don’t let neural networks calculate tax liabilities, validate balance sheet invariants, or simulate regulatory counterfactuals inside a flat latent vector.

A common pushback from the pure deep-learning camp is that scaling foundation models, or adopting modern Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) — world models that learn internal simulations directly from raw data — will eventually let neural networks internalise domain rules without explicit symbolic schemas like Standard Business Report Modeling (SBRM) or Logical English.

A paper published last month in Transactions on Machine Learning Research (TMLR, October 2026) by Paolo Di Prodi, “Mind the Ladder: A Benchmark for Level 1–3 Causal Reasoning in World Models”, is a useful, concrete challenge to that claim. It doesn’t settle the debate on its own — it is one benchmark, in a deliberately simple environment — but it makes the failure mode measurable, and the failure mode it measures is exactly the one that matters in accounting.

Here is what Di Prodi found, what it does and doesn’t show, and why it supports the architecture we are building.

1. “Emergent understanding” vs. Pearl’s Ladder

Modern latent world models (such as LeWorldModel, or LEWM) are often evaluated using Violation-of-Expectation (VoE) paradigms. When a physical rule is violated — an agent teleporting through a solid wall, say — the model’s latent prediction error, its “surprise”, spikes, whereas superficial visual changes produce very little surprise. Proponents often read this as emergent understanding of the underlying rules.

Di Prodi asks a sharper question, grounded in Judea Pearl’s Ladder of Causality:

  1. Level 1 — Association ($P(y \mid x)$): Seeing. Recognising statistical correlations in observational data (“What does a symptom tell me about a disease?”).
  2. Level 2 — Intervention ($P(y \mid do(x))$): Doing. Predicting the effect of an action (“What if I do $X$?”).
  3. Level 3 — Counterfactuals ($P(y_{x^*} \mid x, y)$): Imagining and auditing. Reasoning about what would have happened under different conditions, given what was actually observed (“What if I had acted differently, holding the rest of the world constant?”).

Pearl’s Causal Hierarchy Theorem (formalised by Bareinboim et al.) says that, in general, data from a lower rung is not sufficient to answer questions on a higher rung without additional causal assumptions. Di Prodi’s argument is that a world model’s “surprise” score is essentially a Level 1/Level 2 quantity: a model can achieve low prediction error and convincing VoE surprise by learning associative heuristics (“wall $\rightarrow$ no passing”) while still failing the Level 3 tests in his benchmark.

In accounting, tax compliance and statutory audit, many of the questions that matter most are Level 3 questions:

  • What would the Division 7A deemed dividend have been if the loan had been put under a complying s 109N written agreement before lodgment day?
  • Would CGT event A1 have fallen in the prior income year if the contract date had preceded 30 June?
  • Does reclassifying a ledger narrative change the underlying statutory obligation?

2. The “Glitched Hue” confound: how models latch onto shortcuts

To test whether latent world models learn true mechanisms or spurious shortcuts, Di Prodi introduces the Glitched Hue Two Room experiment:

  • The true causal mechanism ($X \rightarrow Y$): stepping on a $1 \times 1$ white teleport pixel ($X$) moves the agent from Room 1 to Room 2 ($Y$).
  • The spurious confounder ($W$): during training, all 2,882 teleportation events occur in blue rooms, and none in green rooms.

From an observational network’s point of view, room colour and the true mechanism are perfectly correlated. A global background hue is a far easier signal to pick up than a single-pixel trigger, and the paper finds the model leaning on hue as a load-bearing shortcut.

In the paper’s double-blind test (Option C+A) — reversing the confound at test time so teleportation happens in a green room, while masking the direct visual cue so the model must rely on its internal state — the model’s causal diagnostics fail. Its Abduction–Action–Prediction (AAP) surprise ratio crosses the paper’s failure threshold ($1.240$ ratio-of-means; $1.23 \pm 0.07$ per episode across seeds), and the latent representation shows hue entangled with other factors.

The accounting equivalent of “room hue”

Financial data is full of blue-room confounds. In historical general ledgers, an advance to a shareholder is usually posted to an account called "Director Loan" or "Shareholder Drawings".

A model trained end-to-end on historical filings can learn the shortcut:

$$\text{Account label: "Director Loan"} \implies \text{Division 7A applies}$$

Now take the edge case an auditor actually cares about (the Option C reversal): a payment to an associate of a shareholder sitting in "Trade Creditors – Sundry" or "Subcontractors". A label-driven model can miss it — and can equally raise a false positive on a genuine supplier payment that was miscoded to "Director Loan".

This is an analogy, not something Di Prodi tested on ledgers. But it’s the reason our rules are written the way they are. A Logical English rule for Division 7A conditions on the legal parents — whether the payer is a private company, whether the recipient is a shareholder or associate, whether there is a distributable surplus, whether a complying agreement exists. The ledger label doesn’t appear in the body of the rule.

The honest caveat: that only protects the reasoning step. The facts the rule runs on — “this recipient is an associate”, “this payment is a loan” — still have to be extracted from messy ledgers and documents, and that extraction is done by the probabilistic layer. The hue problem doesn’t vanish; it moves upstream to the Fano Classifier and our extraction agents. That is why we test them with Option C+A-style confound reversal (see section 5), and why extracted facts cross an operator-approval boundary before the rules ever see them.

3. Single-factor leakage vs. SBRM structural invariance

The paper’s most striking findings are in Section 3.5 (Single-Factor Leakage) and Section 3.6 (Validation Against Ground-Truth Counterfactuals).

To ask a latent world model a counterfactual question in latent space ($z \in \mathbb{R}^{192}$), you run an AAP cycle: encode the factual state as $z_{fact}$, shift it along the hue direction ($z_{cf} = z_{fact} + \delta v$) to simulate $do(\text{hue} = \text{green})$, and predict the next state.

Di Prodi measures whether shifting only hue leaves unrelated variables alone — a metric he calls Structural Invariance Error ($InvErr$) — and reports two failures:

  1. Cross-factor leakage: intervening on hue perturbs the model’s internal estimate of the episode step index ($InvErr/\text{std} = 1.18$) and distance to target ($InvErr/\text{std} = 0.90$) — close to a full standard deviation each.
  2. Counterfactual drift: compared with a true counterfactual rollout from the simulator, $z_{cf}$ lands about $6.1\text{–}6.2\times \|\delta v\|$ from the true counterfactual state, and the latent shift underestimates the model’s true counterfactual surprise by 22× to 44×.

By analogy, consider asking a latent model an advisory question — “What happens to our net tax position if we choose the small business CGT roll-over?” If the intervention behaves the way Di Prodi’s did, it could bleed into quantities that should be untouched: share capital, the depreciation schedule, opening retained earnings. Nobody has measured this on a financial model — but it is precisely the property an advisory engine must not have.

What SBRM + Prolog give us instead

In Section 6.8 (Oracle Calibration), Di Prodi builds an idealised reference point (true_cause_predict) — a symbolic function that reads only the true variables (dist_to_pad and teleport_enabled) and ignores hue. On that oracle the AAP surprise ratio sits at its ceiling of $1.0000 \pm 0.0000$ with no cross-factor leakage.

That symbolic oracle is close to how SBRM and Logical English already work:

  • SBRM replaces the flat vector with typed hypercubes, dimensions and signed trial-balance movements.
  • SWI-Prolog and Logical English apply an intervention as an explicit change to the fact base inside an isolated snapshot (snapshot/1). The change can only propagate through the rules that actually reference it.

That gives us something a latent shift can’t: structural invariance by construction. If no rule connects a statutory election to the depreciation pool, changing the election cannot change the pool. We then verify it on every counterfactual run, by checking that the invariants we track — cash, settled movements, locked prior-period balances, trial-balance zero-sum — are unchanged (the companion post shows the check).

To be precise about the limits: that is a zero-leakage guarantee for the invariants we choose to check, given correct rules and correct input facts. It is not a proof that the rules themselves state the law correctly — that is a separate question, and it’s what our practitioner review bounties exist to test.

4. Why scaling alone is unlikely to fix observational blindness

Would a bigger model fix this — moving from a 5.7M-parameter ViT-Tiny to a 307M-parameter ViT-Large, or to a multi-billion-parameter transformer?

In Section 7.1, Di Prodi argues that scale alone doesn’t close the gap, for three reasons:

  1. The training objective can’t tell the shortcut from the cause. Standard predictive losses minimise error over the observational distribution, $\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}[\|P(E(o_t), a_t) - E(o_{t+1})\|^2]$. When two features are perfectly correlated in training data, conditioning on the shortcut reaches the same minimum loss as conditioning on the true cause. More capacity doesn’t change which one the loss prefers.
  2. Observational causal discovery can get it backwards. Running the standard PC causal-discovery algorithm on the experiment’s tabular data (Section 6.6) doesn’t just fail — it confidently infers teleported → hue, i.e. that teleporting makes the room blue.
  3. Flat latent vectors carry no causal structure. Every dimension can influence every other, so nothing in the architecture prevents entanglement.

This is an argument plus evidence from a controlled environment, not a theorem about all future models. Interventional data, structured architectures or explicit causal training could all change the picture, and Di Prodi discusses some of these. But the direction he proposes is telling. In Section 7.2 (Pathway #4) he writes:

“Ultimately, replacing the flat 192-dimensional embedding with an explicit Structural Causal Model (SCM) provides the strongest guarantee… this requires learning both a disentangled representation (a small DAG of latent groups) and an identified causal graph where the predictor conditions only on parent nodes.”

In tax, we don’t have to learn that causal graph. Parliament has written most of it down. Our job is to encode it faithfully and have practitioners check the encoding.

5. How we use Mind the Ladder at LodgeiT Labs

Di Prodi’s benchmark gives us a concrete way to test our own boundary, not just an argument for it:

Layer in LodgeiT stack Role in Pearl’s hierarchy How we apply Di Prodi’s framework
Fano Classifier & extraction agents Level 1 perception → candidate facts Tested with Option C+A-style confound reversal: we hide shareholder advances inside sundry trade accounts (“green rooms”) and mask surface labels, to check our extractors pick up structural relationships rather than text correlates. This is where the hue risk now lives.
Brain Pattern (Proofing 0 & 1) Graph integrity & coherence Keeps system memory and statutory contracts anchored in Git-backed Markdown, YAML frontmatter and Prolog coherence checks, so they can’t drift silently.
SBRM + Logical English (ClawDog) Level 2 interventions & Level 3 counterfactuals Uses an explicit, human-readable rule graph in place of a flat latent vector. Runs AAP cycles deterministically in SWI-Prolog, with invariants checked on every run.

Surprise is not understanding, and correlation is not statute. Pairing probabilistic extraction in front with SBRM, Logical English and SWI-Prolog behind an operator-approval firewall means we don’t have to hope a world model climbs Pearl’s Ladder of Causality. We write the ladder down, have it checked, and test the one place the shortcut can still get in.
