The programme

From bounties to ownership: where this is going

See also How we work, How we pay, How we get quality. This page describes intent. The parts that touch professional regulation are themselves under review (Bounty #15); nothing here is an offer.

Today

A bounty is a one-off. You review, you find, perhaps you sign; you are paid; the file improves; the relationship ends until the next bounty. That is the right way to start, and it is how every file on this site has been built. It has one weakness: the people who made a file trustworthy have no stake in it staying trustworthy.

Where we want to get to

Rule files will be used. Tax Genii will answer questions with them; other software will call them through a metered interface; each use will be a small, counted event tied to the exact file hash that answered. Once use is counted, it can be shared.

The intent is that the people who make a file trustworthy hold a share in its use:

  • The maintainer who wrote the file and keeps it current when the law changes earns on every use while the file is current and signed, and stops earning when it lapses. Upkeep is paid for by the same stream that pays for authorship.
  • The signers who put their credential against a hash earn a share on every use of that hash for its effective period. The share accrues into escrow and vests only if no serious defect is upheld against that hash in the period. A defect found later is paid to the finder out of that escrow. A careful signature earns; a careless one funds the person who caught it.
  • The finder remains the best-paid act per hour, because finding is what the whole structure exists to reward.
  • The programme keeps a share for running the engines, adjudicating disputes, and funding a floor for the long tail of rules that are correct, necessary and rarely used, so that those files are maintained too.

Three rules follow from this and will not change:

  1. Your name goes on the hash in the registry, never on an answer. A caller sees the file's hash and tier; who signed it is public in the registry, not stamped on their calculation. This is the line between attesting to a rule and advising a taxpayer, and keeping it is what lets practitioners take part at all.
  2. A share is paid on use, never on outcome. The same amount whether the answer was a nil gain or a large one. It is a licence fee for an artefact, not a fee contingent on anyone's tax.
  3. Everything lapses. Signatures and shares attach to a hash for an effective period. When the file changes, or the law does, they end, and the new file starts at zero. Nobody earns on stale law.

How we get from here to there

We do not intend to switch the whole programme over on a date. The steps are small and each is useful on its own:

  1. Signatures first. Every rule-file bounty now ends with the signature question, and the registry counts signatures on the deployed hash. This exists today.
  2. Count use. When a file is deployed, every answer records which hash produced it. This is needed for the tier label on answers anyway.
  3. Keep the ledger before paying from it. The programme will record the notional split of each use (maintainer, signers, finders' pool, programme) long before any of it is paid out. Signers will be able to see what their share would have been. This tells everyone, including us, whether the numbers are worth anything before anyone is asked to rely on them.
  4. Settle the legal shape. Whether a signer's share is best paid per use into escrow or as a banded licence fee on a schedule is the question put to legal practitioners in Bounty #15. We will follow that answer.
  5. Offer it to existing signers first. The first shares go to people who already signed a hash under the bounty programme, as an addition to the fee they were paid, not a replacement.
  6. Open authoring last. Letting people outside LodgeiT write rule files for the programme needs the dispute process and the sandboxing of rule files to be proven. It comes after the rest.

How the network pays for itself

Today LodgeiT funds every bounty. The programme becomes self-sustaining when its share of the tolls paid to run rules covers three things: the bounties, the engines, and a floor for the long tail of rules that are correct and rarely used. We will publish that line on the registry and show where we are against it.

The stages are additive. Nothing in an earlier stage is undone by a later one, and the programme can stop at any stage and still be useful.

Fiat on the surface, sats underneath

End users buy Tax Genii and LodgeiT in Australian dollars (or pounds, or US dollars), by subscription or invoice, as they do now. Practitioners are paid in dollars against invoices. Neither ever needs to hold or see anything else.

Underneath, each run of a rule is settled machine to machine over the Lightning network in satoshis, through the metered interface described in Why L402, not x402. That layer is there because it is global, works in fractions of a cent, needs no account, and has no issuer who can reverse a payment, which keeps the audit chain whole from the rule's hash to the payment that ran it. It is the settlement layer of record. The dollar layer on top is what a firm sees, and if the dollar layer were ever removed the engine room would not change.

Global by design

A rule file is registered against its hash, its reasoner version, its jurisdiction and its effective period. A file for another country is a different jurisdiction tag, a different practitioner register to check reviewers against, and otherwise the same programme: the same bounty shapes, the same signature question, the same tiers. Logical English, the language the rules are written in, is maintained by a team that has done the work of writing it in languages other than English, so a rule file for a non-English jurisdiction can be read by that jurisdiction's practitioners in their own words. The programme is built for that from the start, and Australian tax law is where it is being proven.

What we are not promising

That the shares will be large. Use of a rule file is counted in cents, and a share of cents is cents until the volume is real. For a long time the flat fee and the findings bonus will be most of what a reviewer earns, and we would rather say that now than have you discover it. The share is a stake in something that may become valuable, held by the people who made it trustworthy, with the downside pointed at carelessness and the upside pointed at being right. That seems to us the correct shape, and we would rather build it slowly and in public than quickly and alone.
